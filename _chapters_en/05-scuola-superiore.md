I should begin by saying that I hesitated before including this chapter, because some of the episodes I recount drift a bit away from the spirit that inspired this book: recalling pleasant, unusual, and amusing moments in which I was either the protagonist or an amused observer. In the end, however, I thought that real life is not made only of roses, and that even the memories we’d rather not revisit still belong to our story — and therefore deserve a place here.

The Istituto Tecnico Industriale Statale in Brescia had a reputation for being very demanding, with strict and uncompromising teachers. I never imagined, though, that my first encounter with the subjects and with the professors would be so traumatic.

## Geography

Let’s start with geography. At the end of class, the teacher, Prof. S. Mariarosa, told us that she would begin oral examinations in the very next lesson, using the blank maps from the atlas. Gianluigi and I timidly approached her to explain that we didn’t have our atlases with us. Since it hadn’t been requested, we had left them at home in Bagolino, and it would take several days for our parents to send them to the boarding school where we were staying. The teacher made a note of this and told us that we were excused. Everything seemed in order.

Yet at the next lesson, as expected, she started calling students to the blackboard. And who did she call first?

When I heard my name, I felt completely lost — incredulous. My attempts to remind her of what she had told us were useless. There was nothing to be done. I hadn’t studied geography at all because I was counting on the justification she had given, and had used that time to focus on other subjects. Result: 4 (out of 10).

Gianluigi was called next, and he too received a glaring insufficient grade. We were both deeply hurt by what we considered an unfair and difficult-to-accept situation. Nevertheless, with commitment, we eventually managed to recover.

## Technical Drawing

This was — and still is — an important subject for a technical institute. The first lessons covered UNI standards, sheet formats, block-letter handwriting exercises, and so on. I actually enjoyed writing in block letters and believed I was doing quite well… until the first in-class test. The grade? 4.

A decidedly low mark — yet one of the highest in the class. Many received 3s, and even a few 2s. Only a handful of repeat students managed to pass; they must have paid their dues the previous year. What an impact!

Over time, however, I improved so much that even today, whenever something needs to be written neatly, my wife hands it over to me because she says I have beautiful handwriting.

## Mathematics

The math teacher was a man with a rather sad demeanour, yet cheerful in his own way — even likable. I remember him fondly, and one episode made me admire him deeply. Around the middle of the 1964/65 school year, he assigned a written test. I don’t remember the topic, but what I do remember is that it went terribly for everyone. When he handed back the papers, the mood in the classroom was gloomy.

He then said something along these lines: “Boys, the test went badly. But if none of you managed to solve the problem (or the algebraic expressions), it makes me wonder whether I explained it poorly.

So the test is annulled. We’ll go over the topic again, and you’ll have another chance.” We all breathed a collective sigh of relief. It was an absolutely unexpected decision, exceptional — perhaps unique — in that academic environment.

Many years later, in 1974, while on my way to work, I was shocked to read in the newspapers that this same teacher, Prof. Alberto Trebeschi, a man I had always respected, was among the victims of the bombing of Piazza della Loggia in Brescia on May 28. My memory of that school episode — and of his fairness — has remained vivid ever since.

## Electrical Engineering

The third year began with the specialized subjects of our course of study. In the class there was a student whom, for privacy, I will refer to as Andrea — a name that resembles his real surname.

He was the kind of person for whom studying seemed an alien concept. He had no desire to open a book, and his grades were abysmal. None of us understood how he had made it to the third year, as he had come from another section and we didn’t know him well.

One day, the electrical engineering teacher, Ing. Valletti, who spoke with a marked lisped “R,” handed back a test and read the grades aloud. Andrea’s grade was missing. After flipping through a few sheets, he finally read the comment written on Andrea’s paper: “Compito senza tvaccia di evvore.” (“Assignment without any trace of mistake.”)

He paused briefly, during which we all turned, wide-eyed, to look at Andrea, stunned by what we had just heard. “Puvtvoppo, (unfortunately) the content is a bit weak,” the teacher continued. “Grade: zevo.” The assignment had been handed in completely blank.

With Marino, however, the same teacher was a little more generous — but the situation was different. Marino had been called to the blackboard for an oral exam and essentially said nothing. The professor wrote the grade in the register and told him: “I gave you a two: one for the trip to the blackboard, and one for the trip back.” Some of us laughed. Marino didn’t.

## The Oral Final Exam

My oral exam was scheduled for July 21, 1969.

I felt I had done well on the written tests, so I was fairly calm — as calm as one can be the night before such an important exam.
That day remains unforgettable for another extraordinary reason: the Moon landing of the Eagle module, which took place at 04:57 Italian time. I considered it an event of enormous importance and decided I didn’t want to miss it. So I woke up at four in the morning to watch the live broadcast.

Of course, it wasn’t the best plan for someone who was supposed to be focused and well-rested for an exam just a few hours later… but my mind was entirely captured by Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin stepping onto the Moon. Just eight years after Yuri Gagarin became the first man in space, the idea that humans were now walking on our natural satellite fascinated me deeply — almost enough to overshadow the final exam awaiting me that same morning.
