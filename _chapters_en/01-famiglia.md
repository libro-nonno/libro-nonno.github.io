Five siblings, one home full of voices, arguments, laughter, and adventures. Graziano, born in September 1941; Flavio, in August 1943; Annamaria, on the first day of 1947; I, Pierluigi, in September 1949; and finally, Angiolino, the youngest, born in March 1951. We were the children of Alessandro and Ernestina, two simple people with a strong sense of family.
In these pages we’ll all appear, more or less directly. Our childhood was a little world of its own, and in that world each of us had a role. One person sadly missing from these memories is Adelmina, the eldest, who died at age seven from a poorly treated pneumonia, before I was born.

## Swaddled

I have no direct memory of what I’m about to recount. I was told this story many times, with that half-serious, half-amused tone reserved for family tales passed down through decades.
I was only a few months old, a tiny bundle wrapped tightly as was customary back then. I had been entrusted—perhaps with excessive optimism—to the care of my brother Flavio, who was just six.

Flavio loved me, that much is certain. But reliability was not his strong suit. He had the habit—maybe as a game, maybe to feel grown-up—of tossing me into the air. Once, then again, and again. Suspended for a moment in mid-air, I probably laughed.
Until, in the most predictable way, a moment of distraction and… I landed. I don’t know if there was a thud, or a cry, or that silent resilience only infants seem capable of.

Sometimes I wonder: could that have been the very moment that shaped me for life?

## Skipping Preschool

Skipping preschool at four years old? Impossible! And yet…
Back then, our village was a place where everyone knew each other. Children went to school on their own, crossed the streets without fear, carrying their little snack baskets. Graziano and Flavio, six and four, walked every day along the kilometre that separated our home from the preschool on the far side of town.
But one afternoon, when they returned home, our mother noticed something odd: one of the snack baskets was still untouched. She asked for explanations. Graziano hurried to defend himself: he had eaten his. And Flavio? “I don’t know, I didn’t see him at preschool.” Strange. Very strange.

Flavio came home a little later, exhausted and starving. Mother looked at him with the eyes of someone who already knows the truth: “Where were you?”
After some hesitation, little Flavio confessed: he hadn’t gone to preschool. Tempted by the sunshine, he had decided to take a day of freedom. With his friend Aldo, he had settled in a nearby meadow and spent the day lying in the grass, dozing under the sky.
“And what did you eat?” Mother asked, puzzled.
Flavio lowered his eyes and whispered, almost guilty:
“I ate… daisies.”

Author’s note: When writing this episode, I tried searching for an expression equivalent to “marinare l’asilo” (“skipping preschool”). Even online dictionaries give up: apparently the idea itself is unthinkable. Perhaps that day truly belongs in the record book — a feat no modern child will ever match.

## The Balloon

One day, our father returned from Brescia carrying two simple but wonderful gifts: two red balloons shaped like ducks. One for me, the other for Angiolino. I was six, he was four. As soon as we received them, we ran outside to play.

My balloon danced lightly between my fingers until—BOOM! — it burst with a sudden explosion. The sound was enormous. I was struck by a deep, childlike sadness. And jealousy soon followed.
I looked at Angiolino, still holding his perfect, intact balloon. A sly, poisonous thought crept into my mind. I ran to him with fake enthusiasm: “You should’ve heard the noise mine made! It was amazing! Come on, let’s pop yours too!”
He hesitated, unsure. But I insisted, using my most persuasive tone. Eventually, he nodded. I grabbed a little stick, brought it to the balloon and… BOOM!
“What a bang, huh?” I said, forcing a smile.

But Angiolino wasn’t smiling. His eyes filled with tears and he burst into a desperate cry. Mother arrived shortly after, drawn by the commotion. I don’t remember exactly how the day ended, but I do know one thing: that mean little gesture still stings when I think about it. Yes, today I can laugh about it—but always with a thin veil of lingering guilt.

## The School Prize

Annamaria was attending primary school and had taken part in a contest organized by the Càffaro company where our father, Sandro, worked. It was reserved for employees’ children. I don’t remember the task—perhaps an essay or a drawing—but the wonderful news was that Annamaria had won.
A large package arrived at our house filled with all sorts of stationery: pencils, pens, erasers, notebooks, sketchbooks, coloured pencils. Beautiful things! It was a moment of pride for our family, for Annamaria—the only girl among us—and for our father, who received congratulations from his colleagues. Even we boys—Angiolino, Graziano, Flavio, and I—were thrilled by her victory.
But…

The next day, father Sandro received an urgent communication from the company. They explained that there had been a mistake in the ranking, and Annamaria was not the actual winner. The officials expressed regret, apologized profusely, but said they were forced to request the prize back so it could be given to the rightful recipient.

I won’t describe father’s reaction in detail. But I remember his indignation ended more or less with these words:
“How can they ask a little girl to return her prize? Impossible! I’d rather pay for it! They can deduct it from my wages if they want, but they can’t expect me to make her give it back! I’ll speak to my supervisor—and to the director as well! This is cruel! It’s horrible!”
Mother Ernestina said nothing, not wanting to worsen the atmosphere. Angiolino and I stayed silent. And Graziano and Flavio?
They suddenly burst into the room shouting: “It was a joke! You fell for it! We wrote the letter!”
Then they ran outside at full speed to escape father’s wrath. Many hours passed before they dared set foot inside the house again.

## The Fake News Broadcast

It was 1956, and our father Sandro was a proud man, firmly rooted in his political convictions. A loyal subscriber to Avanti! and an active member of the town council, he was well known and respected in the village. At the bar, between one card game and another, he never missed a chance to share his enthusiasm for socialist ideas with his friends.

Graziano and Flavio were studying in Turin at a Salesian school—Graziano in the classical program, Flavio in the technical one. They were home on vacation when they came up with an idea—I’m convinced it was Graziano’s—to play a prank on father.
While father was at work, Flavio secretly tinkered with the household radio and with the telephone line that our father used for work-related calls on the electrical grid. In the end, he managed to connect the radio to the phone. Graziano handled the “literary” part of the prank.

When father came home from work, he turned on the radio. At one point, the announcer’s voice cut in:
“Ladies and gentlemen, we interrupt this program for an important announcement. Today, the Honourable Pietro Nenni, Secretary of the Italian Socialist Party, has been appointed by the President of the Republic to form the new government. Pietro Nenni will be the next Prime Minister. We extend our warmest wishes for this important assignment…”

That was the gist of the message. Imagine Sandro’s reaction.
“What news! Finally! It was about time! Now things will change!” He was beside himself with joy. Mother Ernestina couldn’t stop him from rushing to the bar to share the news with his friends.

But his friends were sceptical. None of them had heard the announcement.
“Sandro, are you sure? Who told you? This sounds unlikely.”
“I heard it myself! They just said it on the radio! Believe me!”
Eventually, given Sandro’s solid reputation, they began to believe him.

A reputation that risked collapsing entirely when, hours later, Graziano and Flavio—laughing uncontrollably—confessed everything. It took father a few days to recover from the blow, but from time to time a smile of pride appeared on his face whenever he recalled his sons’ stroke of genius.
The “radio news prank” soon became a legend in the family.
