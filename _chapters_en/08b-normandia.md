It was 1968 and Flavio was in France. He worked for a Swiss company that had been commissioned to build a plant for the production of heavy water. For those who aren’t into nuclear matters, heavy water, that is D₂O, is used to slow down neutrons in nuclear reactors that produce energy. In short, not a particularly relaxing line of work.

Flavio was in charge of the electrical side of the plant and, by the look of it, this was going to be an adventure lasting quite a few years.

## Flavio’s wedding

During his stay in France, Flavio met Françoise, a lovely local girl. The two got engaged and, without wasting much time, decided to marry.

The date was set for August 1968, and the wedding was to be held in her hometown, Bully-les-Mines, in the Pas-de-Calais department, in the Hauts-de-France region. At that point, all we could do was pack our bags and move the whole family, for a while, to the north of France.

Father and Graziano stayed only two or three days, just long enough for the ceremony and the wedding lunch. Angiolino, Annamaria, Mother Ernestina and I, on the other hand, treated ourselves to a whole week. A real French holiday.

The church ceremony was rather short. The priest’s sermon, luckily, was short for us too, mostly because we didn’t understand a single word. But that didn’t stop us from taking part with enthusiasm: when the others smiled, we smiled; when they clapped, we clapped. In short, we had blended in perfectly, at least in our own opinion.

Then came the lunch. And there, at last, language was no longer a problem: everyone knows you don’t talk with your mouth full.

It began with the aperitif, where Pastis and Ricard could hardly be missing, both strictly aniseed-flavoured. Then came the food, all of excellent quality. But the wines deserve a special mention, above all the one served with the cheeses halfway through the meal. It was a Bordeaux from the year Françoise was born.

Her father, following a tradition fairly common in those parts, had had it bottled in that very year and had kept it carefully, waiting for a special occasion. And this, clearly, was just the right one.

I liked the idea so much that I decided to copy it. In the following years I too kept a few bottles from the years my children, Filippo and Linda, were born, meaning to uncork them one day, on a grand occasion.

Pity that, since they were born, the special occasions have been so many that today those bottles no longer exist. With all due respect to the connoisseurs.

All in all, it was a day we could hardly have forgotten.

## The holiday

Flavio had been given a week’s leave for the wedding and decided to make the most of it by showing us a little of that part of France.

He took us to see some of the most important towns in the area: Lille, Boulogne, Arras, Lens, Metz and Calais.

But there was one thing that, more than anything else, aroused our curiosity: the ocean.

None of us had ever seen the Atlantic. The sea was something we knew, but this was a different sea, at least in our imagination. So Flavio decided we couldn’t go home without having seen it. One morning we set off for the coast.

Two cars, a small family expedition and a destination that, to us, had something exotic about it. When we finally reached the coast and found ourselves facing that immense stretch of water, we all stood looking at it with a certain awe. It was the ocean. That alone was enough to make it special.

## The jellyfish

That day the tide was low and you could walk easily along the water’s edge. It was right there that I saw jellyfish for the first time. I was fascinated.

I had never seen any and, probably, until that moment I hadn’t even had a clear idea that they existed. There were lots of them, of every size: small, medium and, above all, large. And they had beautiful colours, delicate, almost unreal: sea green, sky blue, pale pink, violet. Looking at them, they almost seemed like ornaments left on the beach by the ocean. And I, naturally, thought that going up to touch them was completely harmless. I was just about to do it when my sister-in-law stopped me with a shout that made me jump backwards. The jellyfish were poisonous. What a pity, I thought. They were so beautiful.

## The taste of salt

There are things we know because we’ve been told, because we’ve read them in books or simply because we learned them growing up. Then there are those that, when we try them for ourselves, take on a whole different flavour.

We children, for example, having been to the seaside at summer “colonies”, knew perfectly well that sea water is salty. We had learned it very early, often in the least elegant way possible: swallowing a few mouthfuls while trying to learn to swim.

For Mother Ernestina, however, it was not at all obvious. She had never been to the seaside. The salty water was probably one of those little curiosities she had carried with her for years, without ever having the chance to check it for herself. That day, on the ocean beach, during our walk, we saw her stop.

She bent down, touched the water with one hand and then, with an almost scientific seriousness, brought it to her mouth. She tasted it. She even smacked her lips, just as sommeliers do when they have to judge a wine. Finally she turned to us with a convinced look and delivered her verdict:

“It’s really true! It really is salty!” She did not repeat the experiment. One taste had been enough.

And so, after so many years, Mother Ernestina too had finally verified in person one of the great truths of the world: ocean water really does taste of salt.

## The war remnants of the Normandy landings

The low tide offered us another surprise, this time decidedly less romantic. The ocean kept washing up on the beach the remains of a war that, however distant in time, still seemed present. Between the sand and the water’s edge, in fact, many pieces of ordnance appeared, some exploded and others still intact, remnants of the fighting and of the Normandy landings. The French authorities deal constantly with this silent emergency, relying on bomb disposal units. I, of course, had not the slightest idea how dangerous the thing in front of me could be. So, during our walk, I found a shell, a mortar round. It wasn’t very big, nor very heavy, and I thought it might make an interesting souvenir to take home to Italy. I picked it up and carried it along with me, as calmly as someone who has just found a slightly unusual seashell. On the way home, our two tour guides, Flavio and Françoise, took us to visit the “Normandy American Cemetery and Memorial”. That immense expanse of white crosses was a truly shocking sight. The thought that beneath those crosses lay the bodies of boys who had died at my own age shook me deeply.

As I write these memories, I think back to those crosses, to that shell picked up among hundreds of others on the beach, and I cannot help comparing them with the wars being fought today between Russia and Ukraine and in the Middle East, and asking myself how it is possible that we have learned nothing from the great tragedies of the past.

I let my emotions get the better of me, but let me get straight back to the trip. The problem of the shell came up in the evening, when we got home and started unloading our things from the boot of the Ami 8. Françoise’s father saw the object and went white. He looked at it, looked at me and, judging by his expression, at that moment he must have seriously wondered what kind of family had landed in his house. Very carefully he took the shell, carried it away from the house and called the gendarmerie, who came to collect it. Meanwhile, I was beginning to realise I had really done something foolish. What an embarrassing… I’ll say no more.
