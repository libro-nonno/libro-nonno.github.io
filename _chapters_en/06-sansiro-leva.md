## My first time at the San Siro stadium (Championship 1964/1965)

When I was a boy, I was — and still am — passionate about football. My team has always been AC Milan. I would have given anything to attend a match at San Siro, but at that age, in those years, it felt completely out of reach. I, along with the classmates who had decided to continue their studies, had moved to Brescia to attend high school and were living at the “Convitto Arcivescovile S. Giorgio,” which provided room and board.

Fernando, our peer, had instead chosen to attend a carpentry school in Milan. It wasn’t easy to see each other, but we stayed in touch and on good terms. One day Fernando told us that almost every Sunday he managed to attend Milan and Inter matches without paying for a ticket. “If you come visit me, I can get you into the stadium too, and we’ll watch the match together from the posti distinti section.” The idea thrilled us. After much discussion, only Flaviano and I agreed, and we decided we would go. It was anything but simple. First, we had to get permission from the “padre superiore” to be away on Saturday and Sunday, and then figure out what to do once we arrived by train in Milan. I don’t remember what excuse we used, but in the end we obtained permission. When we reached Milan, since we couldn’t afford a hotel, we had to think about how to spend all the hours before meeting Fernando the next morning. From the central station we decided to start walking toward the stadium, without taking public transportation, following the road signs — at least it would help pass the time.

We soon realized it was no short walk. I don’t have a clear memory of how we spent the night, but there are things I haven’t forgotten: a police officer who, seeing us wandering the streets late at night, invited us into the station office to ask why we were out at that hour and where we were headed; the cold; and the relief we felt when we managed to shelter inside the cab of a dark green Piaggio Ape parked along a street. Morning finally came and—tired and a bit dazed—we met Fernando. Late in the morning we found ourselves in front of the stadium. We were far too early, and the square was completely deserted.

Fernando hadn’t exactly explained how we were supposed to get inside, but since we were already there, we followed his instructions: we just had to climb over the gate, and that was it! He showed us how, and we followed.

Once inside, we were left speechless at the sight of the stadium — completely empty — and the pitch, bright green and perfect. A marvel. We avoided being noticed and waited until people began to arrive: first the ultras hanging up their banners, then all the other spectators. The excitement grew as the stands filled. And when the announcer read the line-ups… “… Maldini (Cesare), Trapattoni, Lodetti, Rivera, Altafini…” A roar exploded. 
The match itself wasn’t extraordinary, but it ended with Milan winning 1–0 thanks to a goal by the Brazilian striker Amarildo, and that alone made all our efforts worthwhile. The trip back to Brescia was much easier: Fernando accompanied us to the central station, taking the brand-new M1 metro — inaugurated just a few months earlier — and then we took the train back to our long-awaited destination. A wonderful adventure for two fourteen-year-old boys!

## The Vespa accident

My classmate Giuseppe and I were attending our fourth year of high school. It was a Thursday in May, the only school day with the afternoon free, so we decided to treat ourselves to a short trip to the lake. Riding on Giuseppe’s beautiful white Vespa 125, we were making our way through the streets of Brescia toward Iseo.

I was sitting on the back seat, trying to light a cigarette. It wasn’t easy, and while struggling with it, a thought rose sharply in my mind: “How stupid of me to get distracted like this… all for a cigarette! “The awareness of the foolishness I was committing was crystal clear and unnerving. Approaching an intersection, I continued thinking: “There — if a car came from the street on the right, I wouldn’t even notice it, and this distraction could be fatal!” A prediction that materialized instantly.  A white FIAT 500 burst into the intersection without slowing down, and the collision was inevitable. Instinctively, I lifted my leg to prevent it from being crushed, planted my foot on the hood of the FIAT 500, and suddenly found myself flying through the air — over a meter off the ground, thrown like a projectile. While airborne, I realized I was going to crash onto the pavement, so I decided to dive toward the ground with my arms stretched out to soften the impact. To cushion the fall further, I thought to roll onto my shoulder, turning my body.

As I carried out the movement, I became aware that I had landed in the middle of the intersection and needed to get out of there immediately to avoid being run over by other vehicles. I pushed myself off with all my strength and ended up on my feet, against the building on the opposite side of the intersection. The driver and passenger of the car that had hit us — having seen me fly in front of their windshield — jumped out immediately and ran toward me to make sure I wasn’t hurt, completely overlooking poor Giuseppe, who was lying on the ground next to his Vespa. He had taken the worst of it and later had to go to the emergency room. Once everything was over, I found myself reflecting on several things that had deeply impressed me:
– the premonition that a car would shoot through the intersection without respecting the stop sign,
– the clarity of mind I managed to maintain,
– the speed with which my brain processed the extremely dangerous situation, guiding me through the right decisions, moment by moment, in just a few seconds.

Fortunately, the accident didn’t result in serious consequences — except that for a long time afterward, every time I had to cross an intersection, I felt a wave of anxiety, even when I was riding on a bus on my way to school or downtown. As I write, I relive the moment again, and the black streak left by my shoe on the hood of the FIAT 500 is still etched vividly in my mind — an indelible trace of that instant.

## Summer Jobs

Where did we go on holiday? To the seaside? No. To the mountains? I was already living there, so that didn’t count. So… what did we do? We went to work. During my middle-school years, I spent my summers helping some family acquaintances who ran the restaurant Locanda del Cavallino. There I lent a hand wherever needed — in the kitchen, at the bar, or doing small tasks — and I felt like a real part of the place. Giving up long days of play with my friends wasn’t always easy, but I was motivated: I wanted to be useful, and I wanted to do things well.

During my third and fourth years of high school, the forestry service offered me and a few boys my age the opportunity to be hired from June to September for temporary work planting trees and shrubs around the village. These activities helped stabilize the terrain and reduce the risk of landslides.

The first year we worked near Valle Dorizzo, at about 1,200 meters of altitude. It was demanding work, made even harder by the effort required just to reach the site. We would leave Bagolino by bicycle, and after roughly eight kilometres of uphill riding, our day began with shovels, pickaxes, and wheelbarrows. The fatigue was real, but so was the satisfaction of earning some money and doing something good for the environment and the valley. After four hours of work, the downhill ride back home felt like freedom: hungry but happy, we sped all the way to the village.

The second year the worksite was set up in Monte Suello, opposite the Garibaldi ossuary. To reach it from Bagolino you rode seven kilometres downhill — but at the end of the day, that same road had to be climbed back up, and you certainly felt it. Fortunately, my friend Silvano got permission from his father to use his MV Agusta, an old but reliable motorbike. Going back home without physical effort really changed the entire day.

One episode stayed etched in my memory. One morning, with the road still wet from the rain, Silvano decided to turn off the engine on the descent to save fuel and let the bike coast in neutral. Everything went fine until, reaching a certain speed, he lost control while turning.
The bike slipped, and so did we; we went sliding along the ground, dragged by the weight of the motorbike, until we ended up at the edge of the road, against the mountainside. The first thing we did was check whether we were hurt — luckily, we were both fine.

But as soon as we got up, we noticed a large reddish stain on the wet asphalt. For a moment, given the colour and size, we feared it was blood. Those were tense seconds as we checked ourselves, trying to understand who had been injured. In reality, it was nothing more than the Sanbitter bottles we had brought in our jacket pockets; they had shattered during the fall. We got back on the road toward the worksite — this time with the engine on and the gears engaged. They were tiring jobs, far from home, and the pay was modest. And yet, the satisfaction and the company made those summers truly special.

## At the cinema

There weren’t many entertainment options in the village, and the cinema was one of the few chances for fun and leisure. In summer the theatre was always packed, filled with both locals and the many holidaymakers spending their vacations there. One evening, my friends and I decided to go watch the film that was being shown. I don’t remember the title, but it had seemed interesting. We were a lively group and took the central seats in the front rows. At one point, during a tense scene when the room had fallen completely silent, the suspense was abruptly shattered by a loud, prolonged sound echoing through the theatre: “Prrrrrrrrrrrrr…!” Yes — a loud fart had suddenly broken the tension, and the entire audience burst into roaring laughter.

Flaviano was the culprit, but with a smiling, perfectly calm expression, he slowly turned his head toward the people sitting behind him, as if the noise had come from there. With that gesture he appeared totally innocent to the rest of the spectators and, very slyly, shifted the blame onto the person sitting right behind him. Robertino, feeling unjustly accused, reacted instantly, hurling insults at Flaviano. We couldn’t make out exactly what words he used because they were drowned out by the thunderous laughter that took a long time to fade. It took months before the two of them spoke again and became friends like before.

## The Graduation

My brother Graziano, after finishing classical high school, enrolled in the Faculty of Letters and Philosophy at the Catholic University of Milan. He had earned a scholarship which allowed him to live at the Augustinianum College, near Piazza Sant’Ambrogio. To keep both the scholarship and his place in the college, one had to pass all exams each year and maintain a high grade average. Graziano was determined to earn his degree, and he succeeded without ever failing a single exam.

We were all extremely proud of him — and for good reason: in our village, he would become the first university graduate whose father was a worker. The date set for his thesis defence was July 6, 1965.

Our father, Sandro, arranged the trip from Bagolino to Milan with Mr. Renzo, the local taxi driver. It was quite an expense, but none of us wanted to miss the event in which Graziano would finally receive his prestigious degree. Only Flavio could not attend because he had recently moved to Switzerland for work. Renzo dropped us off at the university entrance and, while everyone else got out of the beautiful blue FIAT 1400, I stayed in the car with him for a moment to understand where he planned to park so that I could find him easily after the ceremony. Once that was clear, I headed toward the entrance as well. Inside, I immediately felt lost — I had no idea where to go.

While trying to orient myself, I saw a tall man in a black gown approaching. Thinking he was a priest, I asked: “Excuse me, Father, could you please tell me how to reach the aula magna?” He smiled, amused, and kindly showed me the way. A bit late, I realized that although I was in a Catholic university, the man was not a member of the clergy but a professor. Eventually, I reached the hall and managed to attend Graziano’s thesis defence before those solemn, robed professors.

To me, the title sounded anything but simple: “The Exclamatory Infinitive in Latin.” Such an unusual topic was, in itself, a guarantee of an excellent evaluation. Graziano graduated with 110 cum laude! A result fully and truly “conquiso*”.   With this word, I’m sure I’ll make Graziano smile —

*(past participle of conquìdere, a rare and fascinating Italian verb, perfectly suited both to the thesis subject and to the one who developed it).

Note: 
Many figures destined to become major players in Italian politics passed through the Augustinianum College, including Amintore Fanfani, Ciriaco De Mita, Romano Prodi, Tiziano Treu, and Giovanni Maria Flick. One of Graziano’s fellow residents was Mario Capanna, who even then stood out for his fiery protests, to the point of being expelled from the university and forced to leave the college.
Graziano chose the path of teaching in classical high schools; he served as a Latin instructor at the Catholic University and authored various publications, especially in Latin and also on the Bagolino dialect. For fifteen years he was the headmaster of the V. Gambara Institute in Brescia, which, during his leadership, became the largest school in Lombardy in terms of student numbers.

## The Recommendation

A recommendation is never something flattering to boast about. Those who benefit from one usually keep it to themselves and do not want to talk about it — unless… During my military service in Cecchignola, Rome, the moment arrived for assignment postings: everyone hoped to be sent somewhere near home, but usually those from the north were assigned to the centre–south, and vice versa.

The lieutenant warned us clearly: no requests or recommendations would be tolerated — anyone who tried would be sent to the farthest possible location. I didn’t think this concerned me at all… until I received a letter from my mother. On her own initiative, she had written to the commander asking that I be assigned to the Brescia district. Immediately, I imagined the worst. 

When the official announcement of postings began, I waited a long time, anxiously. Finally, I heard my name — and especially the destination: Padua! What a relief! Only later did I find out that my mother’s letter had indeed been taken into consideration; the commander himself had written back to her explaining that he had done what he could to help me. For the first time in my life, a recommendation made me smile and feel grateful. When my fellow soldiers told me how lucky I had been, I would reply with a grin: “Of course — I was recommended by my mother!”
